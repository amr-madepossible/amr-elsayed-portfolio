Three problems, one headline

Timing€80K

Revenue has not vanished. It is a period-timing issue in the fictional model.

Mapping€60K

The transaction exists but is associated with the wrong structure.

Allocation€40K

The value exists but is reported against the wrong business structure.

Management conclusion

Do not tell leadership “we lost €180K.”The first conclusion is that there is an explained €180K variance. Investigation and corrective action remain required, but the sample contains no evidence of a commercial loss.
Decision readout

The inexperienced conclusion is “we are €180K short.” The operational conclusion is “we have a €180K unexplained variance.” Once decomposed, €80K is timing, €60K is mapping, and €40K is allocation.

In this fictional scenario, all €180K is explained and the identified commercial loss is €0. The work is reconciliation and correction, not panic.

Why the bridge matters

A financial-control model should convert a scary headline into a sequence of testable causes.

1 · Anchor expected valueStart with the €5M commercial expectation used for the scenario.
2 · Compare to reported resultQuantify the €180K gross variance.
3 · Classify the varianceSeparate timing, mapping, allocation, and any genuinely unexplained residual.
4 · Correct the recordAssign each cause to the team that can validate and correct it.
5 · Re-run the tie-outClose the loop only when the control totals reconcile again.
Connection to the rest of the account

Revenue accuracy sits downstream from the customer and sales story.

A delayed expansion, a billing issue, a mapping error, or a period-timing difference can all create a number that looks like commercial deterioration. Reconciliation keeps the management narrative anchored to what the records actually prove.
